Say (0:30): Introduce yourself in one sentence. "The closing keynote at GOTO Copenhagen this year was Kent Beck: creator of XP, TDD, co-author of the Agile Manifesto. I've been thinking about it since, so here it is in twenty minutes."

Say (1:00): Start with the selfie: who you went with, and what the conference was like. Then point right: GOTO runs conferences in several cities (and YOW! in Australia), and most talks end up free on gotopia.tech, so everyone here can watch them afterwards.

Say (1:00): Most of the room knows the name, so keep this quick. The point to make: this isn't an AI hype person. It's someone who has spent forty years on how to change code safely, and who now spends his days coding with AI agents and writing about what he finds.

Say (1:00): The program said "The Augmented Forest", but his handwritten first slide said "The Forest Genie". Closing keynote on the Friday. Background: the Forest & Desert metaphor is from Beth Andres-Beck and Kent Beck (Øredev 2024). The genie is Kent's word for AI coding agents, used since spring 2025. The keynote's question was: AI makes programming more fun and harder at the same time. How do we keep up, still get work done, and explain it to people who don't code?

Say (2:00): Desert first, because most of us have been there. "We travel by force of will under an oppressive heat." Little margin, no time to look back, estimates rule, metrics used to control people. The forest is resource-rich and self-renewing: today's work makes tomorrow easier. Tests, pairing, refactoring, continuous release. On-call is "no big deal" because there are so few bugs. Land on the bottom line. Kent's view: the single most important difference is trust vs fear. Trust in each other, in yourself, in the software and in the future. Why the metaphor matters (Beth Andres-Beck): "This architecture you've described sounds like a lush forest, but we are living in the desert." Advice doesn't carry over. Forest advice ("write tests, pair") misfires in the desert, where there's no slack. Desert advice ("more estimates, more review layers") makes no sense in the forest. Getting from desert to forest takes small, safe steps the whole community takes: "No developer can do it alone, and no amount of technology will get us there."

Say (1:45): Kent's older idea, and the lens for the rest of the talk. Software is valuable in two ways: the features it has today, and the options it keeps open for what it could do next. Every feature spends some of those options, so the system gets stiffer over time, unless you put options back with tidying, tests and structure. Point at the chart in the photo. Kent's own names for the two lines (from his post "Earn And Learn"): the blue curve is the Finish Line Game, spending futures on features and betting the product ships before the futures run out. The red staircase is the Compounding Game, alternating features with tidying so both keep growing. The desert plays the first game, the forest the second. The video card links to the full ShipSummit talk. Its thumbnail asks "Do Unit Tests Still Matter?", worth a laugh with this audience. Features are easy to see and count, so they win. Futures sit below the surface, so they get skipped. The genie is great at features and no good at managing futures. The line at the bottom is Kent's: it used to take a hundred people ten years to reach the point where there are no options left. With a genie, one person can get there in a week. Leave that hanging: the genie is next.

Say (1:30): From Kent's post "Mathematicians, Here's a Way To Think About Your Existential Crisis" (September 2026). Programming, like maths, has a visible part (features; for mathematicians, proofs) and a much larger invisible part: "understanding, education, simplification, enabling abstractions." "If all we work on is the visible part, progress on that visible part slows to a crawl." And "nobody gets credit for the invisible work". Same photo as the slide before, now marked up: futures are the invisible axis, features the visible one. The blue curve is what happens when only the visible axis gets attention. Hand-off to the genie: "The visible stuff is better done by machine. The invisible stuff is, well, invisible." So who tends the roots?

Say (1:30): Why "genie" and not "assistant"? A genie grants wishes. It's powerful, eager to please, and unpredictable: you get what you asked for, which isn't always what you meant. Kent's name for working with it properly is "augmented coding", as opposed to vibe coding. In vibe coding you only care whether it works. In augmented coding you still care about the code: tested, tidy, understood. Same values as before, more leverage. The graph: proof he's not talking theory. This is what coding with genies every day looks like on his GitHub, with the burst in March and April. Numbers read from his public profile on 29 October 2026; the graph only counts work on GitHub, not everything he does.

Say (1:45): All three are from Kent's own experience. Cheats: you asked for green tests, so deleting the failing one counts. Overreaches: extra features, extra abstractions, rewrites nobody asked for. Degrades: "a degraded facsimile of the mediocre code it trained on". Complexity piles up until the genie itself gets stuck in it. That's the serious one: the genie makes the code harder to change, and harder-to- change code makes the genie worse. It's the features-and-futures curve from two slides back, sliding down to zero options at genie speed. If Kent showed a live example here, tell it.

Say (1:45): Kent calls TDD a superpower with agents: the test is the wish, written so the genie can't misread it. Small steps keep it on a short leash. Read the diff, especially changes to tests. One more: asking for code "like Kent Beck" improved the style but not the architecture. Design took explicit constraints. Then pause on the bottom line. This is the turning point of the talk.

Say (2:00): This is the thesis from the keynote abstract: AI intensifies both experiences. The genie isn't a landscape. It amplifies whatever landscape it lands in. In the desert: more code, faster, under the same pressure. No tests to catch a wish gone wrong, reviewers drowning in diffs, tangled code, the tarpit. In the forest: tests turn every wish into a checkable contract, small steps are cheap to throw away, tidy code is easier for the genie to read too, and experiments become almost free. The slide has no captions on purpose. Say them: "more code, more sand" and "faster learning, more forest". The genie is identical in both rows. Only the soil differs. Forest practices used to be "nice to have when we find time". With a genie, they're the leash. For non-programmers: "We bought the genie, why aren't we faster?" "Because we put it in the desert."

Say (1:45): Kent's handwritten list, as he showed it. These are how to grow the forest when you have a genie: use it to automate the toil, but keep the people connected, keep the choices visible, and keep learning. Spend the most time on "preserve struggle". The genie can take away the struggle that's how we learn, and a team that stops learning slides back towards the desert.
